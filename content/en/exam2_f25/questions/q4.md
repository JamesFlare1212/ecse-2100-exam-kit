## Question

Two conducting spheres of radius $a$ are located on the $x$-axis: One sphere is located at $x=-d$ and has total surface charge $Q$, while the other sphere is located $x=d$ and has total surface charge $-Q$.
A diagram of this system is shown below.

## Guide

This problem combines vector field superposition, electric force and path independence of electrostatic potential.

**Given：** Two conducting spheres of radius $a$ are centered at $x=\pm d$ with charges $+Q$ and $-Q$. The observation point is $P=(0,0,z)$; the potential-difference question runs from the origin to $P$.

**Find：** Start with the exterior field of one sphere, find the combined field and force on a test charge, and set up the potential integral with an explanation of path independence.

**Start here：** Under the exterior-sphere field model used by the exam, write each source-to-observer displacement vector before adding components. Add vectors rather than magnitudes; the later source note discusses the model's scope.

## Part a

First, consider a conducting sphere of radius $a$ and total surface charge $Q$ located at the origin. Find the electric field due to this sphere for $r>a$ (outside the sphere).

### Solution

Use [Gauss’s law](formula:gauss) from the crib sheet:

$$
\oiint_{S_R}\vec{D}\cdot d\vec{S}=Q_{\mathrm{enc}}=Q
$$

Given an isolated conducting sphere of radius $a$ and charge $Q$, find its exterior field. Choose $R>a$ and use the [constitutive relation](formula:constitutive) to substitute the radial field and [spherical area element](formula:geometry). At fixed $R$, $\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)$ is independent of the angles. Integrate in order using $\hat r\cdot\hat r=1$, $\cos\pi=-1$ and $\cos0=1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_D&=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi(\epsilon_0E_r(R)\hat r)\cdot(\hat r R^2\sin\theta)\,d\theta d\phi\\
&=\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi(\hat r\cdot\hat r)\sin\theta\,d\theta d\phi\\
&=\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)\int_0^{2\pi}\left(\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\right)d\phi\\
&=\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)\int_0^{2\pi}[-\cos\theta]_0^\pi\,d\phi\\
&=\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)\int_0^{2\pi}[(-\cos\pi)-(-\cos0)]\,d\phi\\
&=\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)\int_0^{2\pi}2\,d\phi\\
&=2\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)[\phi]_0^{2\pi}\\
&=2\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)(2\pi-0)\\
&=4\pi\epsilon_0R^2E_r(R)=Q
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing by the spherical flux factor and writing $R$ as $r$ gives

$$
\vec{E}=\boxed{\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\hat{r}}
$$

## Part b

Find the total electric field due to both of the conducting spheres in the figure above at the observation point $P=(0,0,z)$. You must express your answer in terms of cartesian coordinates.

### Solution

Use the [Coulomb field](formula:coulomb) from the crib sheet and superposition, following the official solution’s point-charge approximation at each sphere’s centre:

$$
\vec{E}_i(P)=\frac{q_i}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\vec{R}_i}{|\vec{R}_i|^3},\qquad\vec{E}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_i\vec{E}_i
$$

Given $+Q$ at $(-d,0,0)$ and $-Q$ at $(d,0,0)$, find the field at $P=(0,0,z)$. First form the displacements from each charge to P:

$$
\begin{aligned}\vec{r}_+&=(-d,0,0),&\vec{R}_+&=(d,0,z)\\\vec{r}_-&=(d,0,0),&\vec{R}_-&=(-d,0,z)\end{aligned}
$$

Substitute each charge, displacement and common distance into the Coulomb field and add the Cartesian components:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\displaystyle |\vec{R}_+|=|\vec{R}_-|=\sqrt{d^2+z^2},\qquad\vec{E}_+=\frac{Q(d\hat{x}+z\hat{z})}{4\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\\[4pt]&\displaystyle \vec{E}_-=\frac{(-Q)(-d\hat{x}+z\hat{z})}{4\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{Q(d\hat{x}-z\hat{z})}{4\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\\[4pt]&\displaystyle \vec{E}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{Q[(d+d)\hat{x}+(z-z)\hat{z}]}{4\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\end{aligned}
$$

The $z$ components cancel and the $x$ components add, giving

$$
\vec{E}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\boxed{\frac{Qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{x}}
$$

## Part c

A point charge with charge $-Q$ is now placed at observation point $P$. What is the force on the point charge due to the two conducting spheres?

### Solution

Use the [force](formula:coulomb) on a point charge in an external field:

$$
\vec{F}=q_{\mathrm{test}}\vec{E}_{\mathrm{ext}}
$$

Given a test charge $-Q$ at P, find its force. Substitute the external field from (b), retaining the approximation that neglects redistribution of charge on the spheres:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\displaystyle q_{\mathrm{test}}=-Q,\qquad\vec{E}_{\mathrm{ext}}=\frac{Qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{x}\\[4pt]&\displaystyle \vec{F}=(-Q)\frac{Qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{x}\end{aligned}
$$

This gives a force along $-x$:

$$
\vec{F}=\boxed{-\frac{Q^2d}{2\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\hat{x}}
$$

## Part d

Set up an integral for calculating the potential difference between the origin $O=(0,0,0)$ and the observation point $P=(0,0,z)$. You do not need to solve it!

### Solution

Use the [potential-difference line integral](formula:potential) from the crib sheet:

$$
V(P)-V(O)=-\int_O^P\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}
$$

O and P lie on the $z$ axis. Set up the potential-difference integral using $\zeta$ as the path variable and $z$ as the endpoint:

$$
\vec{r}(\zeta)=(0,0,\zeta),\quad 0\le\zeta\le z,\quad d\vec{l}=\hat{z}\,d\zeta
$$

Substituting the field from (b) gives the required integral; the field is perpendicular to the path tangent.

$$
V(P)-V(O)=-\int_0^z\frac{Qd\,\hat{x}\cdot\hat{z}}{2\pi\epsilon_0(d^2+\zeta^2)^{3/2}}\,d\zeta=\boxed{0}
$$

## Part e

In electrostatics, does the path of integration matter when calculating potential differences? Which of Maxwell’s equations for electrostatics tells us this?

### Solution

Use the [electrostatic Maxwell–Faraday equation](formula:conservative) from the crib sheet:

$$
\oint\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}=0,\qquad\nabla\times\vec{E}=0
$$

Given paths $C_1$ and $C_2$ with the same endpoints, determine whether the [potential difference](formula:potential) depends on the path. Close the loop with $C_1$ and reversed $C_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\displaystyle 0=\oint_{C_1-C_2}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}=\int_{C_1}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}-\int_{C_2}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}\\[4pt]&\displaystyle -\int_{C_1}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}=-\int_{C_2}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}=V(B)-V(A)\end{aligned}
$$

This gives

$$
\boxed{\Delta V\ \text{is path independent}}
$$

It depends only on the endpoints.

## Source correction

Model limitation: real conducting spheres polarize one another, so their exact fields are not generally center-point-charge fields. This follows the exam model; it is a useful approximation when $a\ll d$.
