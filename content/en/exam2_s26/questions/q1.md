## Question

As discussed in class, due to the skin effect, current prefers to flow closer to the surfaces of a conductor at higher frequencies. Consider the microstrip line below, which has the following properties:
• Width (in $y$-dimension): $W$
• Height (in $z$-dimension): $h$
• Length (in $x$-dimension): infinite
• Conductivity: $\sigma$

The current density distribution in the microstrip line is given by:
$$\vec J(z)=\begin{cases}J_0\hat x&\text{for }0.4h\le z<0.5h\\0&\text{for }-0.4h<z<0.4h\\J_0\hat x&\text{for }-0.5h<z\le-0.4h\end{cases}$$
Ignore any electric field fringing effects due to the finite width of the microstrip line.

## Guide

This problem connects current-density flux, microscopic Ohm's law and the resistance increase caused by skin effect.

**Given：** The strip has width $W$, thickness $h$ and conductivity $\sigma$, with current along $x$. The given piecewise $\vec J(z)$ is nonzero in the top and bottom surface layers and zero in the middle.

**Find：** Find total current $I$, voltage drop per unit length $V^\prime$ and resistance per unit length $R^\prime$, then explain the effects of conductivity and current distribution.

**Start here：** Identify the current-carrying regions before integrating over a cross-section perpendicular to the current. Then use $\vec{J}=\sigma\vec{E}$ to connect field and voltage drop, and apply the definition of resistance.

## Part a

Calculate the total current $I$ flowing through the microstrip line.

### Solution

Use the [current-flux formula](formula:current) from the crib sheet, retaining the direction of $\vec J$:

$$
I=\iint_S\vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}
$$

Given $\vec J=J_0\hat x$ in the upper and lower layers and zero in the middle, find total current $I$. Use the $yz$ cross-section with normal $+\hat x$:

$$
d\vec{S}=\hat{x}\,dy\,dz,\qquad \hat{x}\cdot\hat{x}=1
$$

Substitute the piecewise current density and integrate over the lower, middle and upper layers:

$$
\begin{aligned}I&=I_{\mathrm{bottom}}+I_{\mathrm{middle}}+I_{\mathrm{top}}\\I_{\mathrm{bottom}}&=\int_{-0.5h}^{-0.4h}\!\int_0^W J_0\,dy\,dz\\I_{\mathrm{middle}}&=\int_{-0.4h}^{0.4h}\!\int_0^W 0\,dy\,dz=0\\I_{\mathrm{top}}&=\int_{0.4h}^{0.5h}\!\int_0^W J_0\,dy\,dz\end{aligned}
$$

Since $J_0$ is independent of $y$, integrate across the width first, then substitute the $z$ limits for each layer. Subtract the negative lower limit in the bottom layer as well:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^W J_0\,dy&=J_0\int_0^W1\,dy=[J_0y]_0^W\\
&=J_0(W-0)=J_0W,\\
I_{\mathrm{bottom}}&=J_0W\int_{-0.5h}^{-0.4h}1\,dz\\
&=J_0W[z]_{-0.5h}^{-0.4h}\\
&=J_0W[(-0.4h)-(-0.5h)]\\
&=J_0W(-0.4h+0.5h)=0.1J_0Wh,\\
I_{\mathrm{top}}&=J_0W\int_{0.4h}^{0.5h}1\,dz\\
&=J_0W[z]_{0.4h}^{0.5h}\\
&=J_0W(0.5h-0.4h)=0.1J_0Wh,\\
I&=(0.1+0+0.1)J_0Wh
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
I=\boxed{0.2J_0Wh}
$$

## Part b

Calculate the magnitude of the potential drop per unit length $V^{\prime}$ of the microstrip line.

### Solution

Use the [potential-difference formula](formula:potential) and [microscopic Ohm’s law](formula:ohm) from the crib sheet:

$$
V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l},\qquad\vec{J}=\sigma\vec{E}
$$

Given $\vec J=J_0\hat x$ in a conducting layer and conductivity $\sigma$, find the potential-drop magnitude per unit length. Substitute into Ohm’s law and integrate along $+x$:

$$
\vec{E}=\frac{\vec{J}}{\sigma}=\frac{J_0}{\sigma}\hat{x},\qquad d\vec{l}=\hat{x}\,dx
$$

Substitute the field and path element into the line integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(\ell)-V(0)&=-\int_0^\ell\frac{J_0}{\sigma}(\hat x\cdot\hat x)\,dx\\
&=-\frac{J_0}{\sigma}\int_0^\ell1\,dx\\
&=-\frac{J_0}{\sigma}[x]_0^\ell\\
&=-\frac{J_0}{\sigma}(\ell-0)=-\frac{J_0\ell}{\sigma}
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the drop magnitude and dividing by $\ell$ gives

$$
V^\prime=\boxed{\frac{J_0}{\sigma}}\quad [\mathrm{V/m}]
$$

## Part c

Calculate the resistance per unit length $R^{\prime}$ of the microstrip line.

### Solution

Use the [resistance definition](formula:resistance) from the crib sheet and divide by length:

$$
R=\frac{\Delta V}{I},\qquad R^\prime=\frac R\ell=\frac{\Delta V/\ell}{I}=\frac{V^\prime}{I}
$$

Using $I$ and $V^\prime$ from (a) and (b), find $R^\prime$. Substitute both results:

$$
I=0.2J_0Wh,\qquad V^\prime=\frac{J_0}{\sigma},\qquad R^\prime=\frac{J_0/\sigma}{0.2J_0Wh}
$$

Cancelling $J_0$ gives

$$
R^\prime=\boxed{\frac{5}{\sigma Wh}}\quad [\Omega/\mathrm m]
$$

## Part d

For the same current density as above, use physical reasoning to explain why increasing the conductivity $\sigma$ would lead to a decrease in the resistance of the microstrip line.

### Solution

Use [microscopic Ohm’s law](formula:ohm) and [current flux](formula:current) from the crib sheet:

$$
\vec{J}=\sigma\vec{E},\qquad I=\iint\vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}
$$

The current-density distribution and cross-section stay fixed. Determine the effect of increasing $\sigma$ on [resistance](formula:resistance) by substituting the fixed $I$ and $E_x=J_0/\sigma$:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\displaystyle I=0.2J_0Wh=\text{constant},\qquad E_x=\frac{J_0}{\sigma}\\[4pt]&\displaystyle R^\prime=\frac{V^\prime}{I}=\frac{J_0/\sigma}{0.2J_0Wh}=\frac5{\sigma Wh}\end{aligned}
$$

This gives

$$
\boxed{\sigma\uparrow\ \Longrightarrow\ R^\prime\downarrow}
$$

Higher conductivity requires a smaller driving field for the same current.

## Part e

For the same total current flowing through the microstrip line, how would the resistance of the microstrip line differ if the current density were distributed uniformly over the cross-section instead of being concentrated near the surfaces? Briefly explain using physical reasoning.

### Solution

Use [current flux](formula:current) and [microscopic Ohm’s law](formula:ohm) from the crib sheet:

$$
I=\iint\vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S},\qquad\vec{E}=\frac{\vec{J}}{\sigma}
$$

Keep total current $I$ fixed and find the resistance for a uniform distribution. Integrate the uniform density over the full $Wh$ cross-section:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\!\int_0^W(J_{\mathrm{uniform}}\hat x)\cdot(\hat x\,dy\,dz)\\
&=J_{\mathrm{uniform}}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\!\int_0^W1\,dy\,dz\\
&=J_{\mathrm{uniform}}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}[y]_0^W\,dz\\
&=J_{\mathrm{uniform}}(W-0)\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}1\,dz\\
&=J_{\mathrm{uniform}}W[z]_{-h/2}^{h/2}\\
&=J_{\mathrm{uniform}}W[h/2-(-h/2)]=J_{\mathrm{uniform}}Wh,\\
J_{\mathrm{uniform}}&=\frac I{Wh}=\frac{0.2J_0Wh}{Wh}=0.2J_0
\end{aligned}
$$

Substitute the new density into Ohm’s law and the [resistance definition](formula:resistance) $R^\prime=V^\prime/I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}&\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{uniform}}=\frac{0.2J_0}{\sigma}=0.2E_{\mathrm{skin}},\qquad V^\prime_{\mathrm{uniform}}=0.2V^\prime_{\mathrm{skin}}\\[4pt]&\displaystyle R^\prime_{\mathrm{uniform}}=\frac{I/(\sigma Wh)}{I}=\frac1{\sigma Wh},\qquad R^\prime_{\mathrm{skin}}=\frac5{\sigma Wh}\end{aligned}
$$

The conducting area increases fivefold, giving one fifth of the original resistance.

$$
R^\prime_{\mathrm{uniform}}=\boxed{\frac15 R^\prime_{\mathrm{skin}}}
$$
