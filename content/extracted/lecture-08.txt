--- PAGE 6 ---
Field Math
 ...but what is a field, anyway? A field:

 • Has a value at every point in space
 •  Could be a scalar field, where every point has scalar quantity (i.e. electric
   potential)
 •  Could be a vector field, where every point has a vector (i.e. electric field,
   magnetic field)
 •  Scalar and vector fields are often related through gradients

Field Math

•  3 Types of coordinate systems we will use for fields:
    • Rectangular
    •  Cylindrical
    •  Spherical

•  Examples of when to use them:
    • Conductive sheets (rectangular)
    • Wires and cables (cylindrical)
    • Spheres (spherical)

•  By choosing our coordinate systems for symmetry reasons, we can make the math
   easier. For instance, both cables and the cylindrical coordinate system have radial
  symmetry.

Orthogonal Coordinate Systems
 •  Regardless of the orthogonal coordinate system, the following relationships
   are always true:
     • The dot product of a unit vector with itself is always 1
                              Ƹ𝑟⋅Ƹ𝑟= ෠𝜙⋅෠𝜙= Ƹ𝑧⋅Ƹ𝑧= ො𝑥⋅ො𝑥= ො𝑦⋅ො𝑦= 1
     • The dot product of a unit vector with any other unit vector in the coordinate system is
     always 0 (orthogonality)
                        Ƹ𝑟⋅෠𝜙= Ƹ𝑟⋅Ƹ𝑧= ෠𝜙⋅Ƹ𝑧= ො𝑥⋅ො𝑦= ො𝑦⋅Ƹ𝑧= ො𝑥⋅Ƹ𝑧= 0

     • The dot product between two vectors is the sum of the products of the individual vector
     components in each coordinate
                  Ԧ𝐴⋅𝐵= 𝐴𝑟𝐵𝑟+ 𝐴𝜃𝐵𝜃+ 𝐴𝜙𝐵𝜙= 𝐴𝑥𝐵𝑥+ 𝐴𝑦𝐵𝑦+ 𝐴𝑧𝐵𝑧= ⋯

     • The magnitude of a vector is the square root of the dot product of the vector with itself

Differential Surfaces and Volumes

• To visualize a dS element:
    • Take the direction that the surface’s normal vector points in and hold it constant (i.e. z)

    • Sweep the other two coordinates through all possible values (i.e. xand y)

    • What shape is traced out by this process? This shape is made of all the dS elements for the
      vector you specified.
